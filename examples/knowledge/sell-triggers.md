# Designing reassessment and exit triggers

Before a decision, specify observable facts that would invalidate the thesis, the date by which a milestone should occur, and any separately chosen price protection. Avoid phrases such as “obvious deterioration” without a measurable condition. Distinguish temporary adversity from a broken mechanism. Explain what would make an exit judgment mistaken, too. A long-term investment and a short-term experiment require different review conditions; a loss should not silently change the position’s original purpose. These are analysis prompts, not automatic orders.

Related: [[bear-case]] · [[sell-review]] · [[decision-quality]]

## Design a trigger before the outcome

| Layer | Template | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | If [business mechanism] fails under [defined condition], reassess | Filing, contract, operational measure or governance disclosure |
| Time | If [milestone] is absent by [date], revisit the original timetable | Dated milestone and the reason its timing matters |
| Price protection | If [explicitly chosen price condition] occurs, review the agreed protection plan | Price source, observation convention and user decision |

A price condition is not a substitute for understanding the business. A long-horizon thesis may survive a quotation decline; a short experiment may have a deliberately different loss tolerance. The user must choose those conditions; this note supplies no default stop-loss percentage.

## Worked fictional example

A fictional supplier is being researched because repeat customers are expected to renew a service contract. The analyst records the renewal date, the disclosure that could confirm it and the cash runway if renewal is delayed. A missed disclosure date triggers a request for evidence; confirmed loss of the contract triggers reassessment of the cash-flow thesis. Neither event tells Argo to place an order. The user weighs the evidence and decides separately.

## Review checklist

- Was the trigger written before the decision, with a timestamp and source assumptions?
- Can another reader determine whether it occurred, without guessing what “bad” means?
- Does the signal concern the mechanism that supports value, or only short-term sentiment?
- Is there an alternative explanation that requires further evidence?
- Are a new review date and remaining risks recorded?
- What observation could show that exiting would itself be mistaken?

## Preserve the decision history

Record the original purpose, trigger, new evidence and revised judgment separately. Do not rewrite a short experiment into a long-term holding solely because it lost money. Conversely, do not force a sale merely to defend an old forecast when new evidence supports a different conclusion. Fisher- and Lynch-inspired reassessment depends on the business story; it is not a universal mechanical exit formula. Argo stages and archives analysis only.
